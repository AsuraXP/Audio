The first warning arrives at 4:17 in the morning. Your phone shivers across the table, and the alert on the screen seems ridiculous: temperatures falling worldwide. Not a storm crossing one country. Not a cold front rolling down from the north. Every weather station, from the equator to the poles, is reporting the same impossible direction. Down.

You pull the blanket tighter and refresh the page. The forecast changes while you watch. Six degrees below normal. Then nine. Then the power flickers, and the apartment goes dark. Outside, the city is still awake. Car horns echo through the streets. A siren passes, then another. Snow begins drifting sideways beneath the streetlights, though the calendar says early autumn.

By sunrise, the news has a name for it: the Long Drop. No one knows why it has begun, or how far the temperature will fall. You only know your window has turned silver with frost, and the pipes in the kitchen have stopped running. The first rule of survival is simple: don’t wait for certainty. Cold steals time while people wait for an explanation.

You fill every bottle and pot while water still flows. You lower the blinds, close doors, and choose the smallest room with no outside-facing windows. A room is easier to warm when it is small, and the blankets, spare clothes, towels, and curtains become insulation. You keep layers loose, not tight: trapped air helps hold heat. You put on dry socks, a hat, and a second shirt, then add a coat only when you must move.

Your phone buzzes again. The city asks everyone to stay indoors, conserve battery, and check on anyone alone. You send one short message to your neighbor across the hall. The reply comes back at once: a frightened thumbs-up, then a photo of a thermometer reading minus twelve. You promise to knock if the corridor stays safe.

At noon, the power fails for good. The building’s heating stops, and the silence feels colder than the wind. You do not light the camping stove in the apartment. You know a flame can use up oxygen, and carbon monoxide can kill without warning. Instead, you seal drafts with towels, keep your phone warm against your body, and turn off anything that drains power. You eat a small meal, drink water, and avoid sweating. Wet clothes lose heat quickly.

You take stock. You count two bottles of water, a few cans, dried fruit, a first-aid kit, and the battery radio. You put the food where both of you can reach it, wrap the water bottles in cloth to slow them freezing, and note how much you have. The number gives you a plan; panic gives you nothing. You keep a written list because the phone battery is precious. You set a timer to check the room, your neighbor, and the radio, then dim the screen. You do not seal every vent or block the building’s airflow. You only reduce drafts around doors and windows, keeping any heater’s required ventilation clear. Each choice is small, but together they turn one cold room into a place you can manage.

A broadcast crackles from the battery radio. Roads are closing. Trains have stopped. The city shelter is open at the old sports hall, but the route crosses a bridge exposed to the wind. You look at the window. Snow has erased the street. The safest choice is to stay put for now, unless your room becomes dangerously cold or someone needs urgent help. Going outside without a plan could turn a bad night into your last.

The hours crawl. You pace gently to keep your blood moving, but you stop before you sweat. You check your fingers, your face, and your neighbor’s door. When the hallway chills, you wrap a scarf over your nose and mouth and make the short trip across. Your neighbor is older, alone, and trying to heat the room with an oven. You switch it off, explain the danger, and bring them back to your insulated room.

There is barely space for both of you, but shared warmth matters. You place a folded blanket between you and the floor, keep your shoes close, and divide the water. You watch for confusion, slurred speech, unusual clumsiness, or uncontrollable shivering. Those are warning signs of hypothermia, not proof that someone is simply tired. If they appear, you call emergency services if possible, move the person gently to warmth, and seek professional help. You do not rub numb skin or use direct high heat.

At dusk, the radio announces that the temperature has dropped another ten degrees across the region. The voice on the broadcast is calm, but the message is not: the cold is spreading faster than the models can explain. Then every station cuts out at once.

You stare at the dead radio. Something taps against the window. Not hail. Not a branch. Three deliberate knocks, from outside, six floors above the street. Your neighbor grips your sleeve. In the blue darkness, you see a tiny light moving across the frozen rooftops.

Your phone lights up with one last signal. A message arrives from the emergency network: “If you can see the lights, do not go toward them.”

Then the signal disappears.

You turn off your screen to save the battery. You listen to the building settle around you, and the cold presses against every wall. Somewhere beyond the glass, the light stops moving. A shadow steps into the glow.

The world has only just begun to freeze. And now, something is moving through the storm.
